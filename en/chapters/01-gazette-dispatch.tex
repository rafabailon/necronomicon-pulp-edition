\section*{The Bell Beneath the Breakwater}
\textsc{Greyhaven, October 3.} A bell rang beneath the harbor yesterday at
half past three, though the old breakwater has stood without a bell since the
great storm of 1849. Three fishermen heard it from the eastern quay. Each
described the note as slow, enormous, and somehow damp.

The harbor master blamed a shifting buoy. Yet no buoy was found missing, and
the sound travelled upward through the soles of boots before it reached the
ear. Seagulls abandoned the roofs in a single white cloud. Even the dogs along
Pilgrim Street refused to cross the square.

\section*{A Notice to Readers}
The Chronicle requests that visitors refrain from scraping green glass from
the seawall. The fragments are not treasure, nor are they, as one excited
schoolboy claimed, the scales of a saint. They are ordinary glass, smooth as
an eyelid and cold even beside a stove.

At the fish market, a chalk mark has appeared on the door of the shuttered
customs house. It resembles a spiral with one broken arm. The watchman has
painted over it twice. By morning the sign returns, paler, and turned toward
the sea.

\section*{The Weather Ledger}
Barometer falling; wind northeast; visibility poor beyond the reef. Captain
Morrow reports a glow below the waterline, blue-green and steady as a street
lamp. The crew lowered a lead weight. It returned warm, its rope neatly cut
below the third knot.

Dr. Elian Voss, visiting the coast to catalogue tide pools, has asked the
newspaper to correct its account. He says the light is probably a colony of
phosphorescent organisms. He says this while refusing to look at the harbor
after sunset.

The Chronicle's compositor has requested reassignment. He says that the
type for the word \textsc{below} keeps appearing upside down in the forme.
The foreman calls it careless work. The compositor has worked here twenty
years and has never once misspelled a headline.

\section*{Editorial}
Readers are advised to keep their lamps trimmed and their doors latched. If
the bell sounds again, do not count its strokes. A count was attempted in
1849, and the official report ends halfway through a sentence.

\textbf{Tomorrow's tide table:} high water at 6:12; low water at 11:47.
The almanac gives no entry for a tide that arrives from inland.
